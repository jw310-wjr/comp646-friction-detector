%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
Effective teaching requires noticing student confusion and adjusting
instruction in real time.  We present a system that automatically detects
\emph{pedagogical friction}: moments where a low-quality instructional
strategy coincides with elevated student confusion.  Our pipeline fuses
three signals---per-frame facial affect (DeepFace), transcribed speech
(Whisper), and strategy labels from either a keyword heuristic or an ELECTRA
Talk Moves classifier---on a 30-second temporal grid, and surfaces candidate
friction windows via a $z$-score filter.  Candidate windows are then passed
to Qwen2.5-VL for multimodal confirmation and alternative-strategy
generation.  We validate the pipeline at two scales: a CPU smoke test on
the TIMSS AU1 \emph{Exterior Angles} clip (5\,min, 3 candidates detected)
and a transcript-only analysis across \textbf{53 TIMSS lessons} spanning
7 countries and both math and science subjects (5\,113 bins total).
The heuristic annotator flags 31.5\% of bins as risky; the ELECTRA
annotator flags 18.9\%, with a negative cross-annotator correlation
($r{=}{-}0.35$), revealing that annotation methodology is itself a critical
design variable in friction detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-quality teaching requires moment-by-moment diagnosis of student
understanding and rapid strategy adjustment~\cite{sherinvanes2009}.
Despite advances in educational NLP---tools such as Tutor
CoPilot~\cite{tutorcopilot} and Edu-ConvoKit~\cite{educonvokit} offer
transcript-based feedback---no existing system jointly models instructional
strategy quality \emph{and} student affect to surface actionable friction
moments during a lesson.  Confusion has been identified as a pivotal
affective state that, when unaddressed, impedes deep
learning~\cite{dmello2012}.  Meanwhile, large vision-language models
such as Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{qwen25vl} now make it feasible to synthesise
video frames, transcripts, and affect signals in a single inference pass.

We introduce a framework that operationalises \emph{pedagogical
friction}---the joint occurrence of a risky instructional strategy and
elevated student confusion---and produces a post-session
\emph{Teacher Friction Report} with precise timestamps and suggested
instructional moves.  Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item An end-to-end multimodal pipeline combining affect, ASR, and
        strategy annotation (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A three-tier strategy annotator hierarchy (ELECTRA $\to$
        Edu-ConvoKit $\to$ heuristic) with graceful fallback.
  \item A large-scale transcript analysis across 53 TIMSS
        lessons~\cite{timss1999video} spanning 7 countries.
  \item An empirical comparison of heuristic vs.\ ELECTRA annotators
        exposing a $r{=}{-}0.35$ disagreement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Automated analysis of classroom discourse has a rich history.
Talk Moves---pedagogical dialogue moves such as \textit{Revoicing} and
\textit{Pressing for Reasoning}---were codified by~\cite{sherinvanes2009}
and later operationalised for NLP by~\cite{talkmovesaaai}, who fine-tuned
ELECTRA on a labelled corpus of math classroom transcripts.
Edu-ConvoKit~\cite{educonvokit} packages this classifier into a reusable
toolkit.  Tutor CoPilot~\cite{tutorcopilot} extends the idea to real-time
tutoring, providing strategy-quality labels that influenced our quality
mapping.

Affect in learning has been studied extensively: D'Mello and
Graesser~\cite{dmello2012} established that confusion, when properly
resolved, promotes deeper learning, but unresolved confusion impedes it.
Their work motivates our use of confusion as the primary affect signal.
DeepFace~\cite{deepface} provides the per-frame affect estimates we use
as a proxy.

On the vision-language side, Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{qwen25vl} represents the
current state of the art in open-weight multimodal reasoning, enabling
our optional fusion stage without proprietary API access.

The closest prior work to ours is the TIMSS Video Study~\cite{timss1999video},
which provided the classroom recordings and transcripts we use for
evaluation but relied entirely on manual coding.  Our system automates
and scales this analysis.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:model} shows the system overview.

\textbf{Vision stream.}
Video frames are sampled at $r{=}2$\,Hz.
DeepFace~\cite{deepface} estimates seven per-frame emotion probabilities,
which we aggregate to a scalar confusion proxy $c_t\!\in\![0,1]$ via a
fixed weight table:
\begin{equation}
  c_t = \sum_{e} w_e \cdot p_t(e),
  \label{eq:conf}
\end{equation}
where $w_e{=}0$ for neutral/happy, $w_e{=}0.85$--$0.9$ for fear/sad,
and $w_e{=}0.45$--$0.5$ for disgust/angry.  A trailing 30-second mean
yields the smoothed confusion timeline $C(t)$.

\textbf{Language stream.}
faster-Whisper~\cite{whisper} transcribes audio with voice-activity-detection
(VAD) filtering.  For transcript-only runs, TIMSS \texttt{.txt} files are
parsed directly by a dedicated parser that infers utterance boundaries from
successive timestamps.  A three-tier strategy annotator hierarchy selects
the best available model at runtime:
(1)~\textbf{ELECTRA} (\texttt{YaHi/teacher\_electra\_small}, 7-class
Talk Moves~\cite{talkmovesaaai}), loaded via HuggingFace
\texttt{transformers};
(2)~the Edu-ConvoKit wrapper~\cite{educonvokit} as secondary fallback; and
(3)~a keyword heuristic as offline fallback.
ELECTRA labels are mapped to quality: \textit{Keeping Everyone Together},
\textit{Revoicing}, and \textit{Pressing for Reasoning} are high-quality;
\textit{Pressing for Accuracy} is high-pressure (risky); \textit{No Move}
is treated as neutral in ELECTRA mode but flagged as unknown in heuristic
mode.

\textbf{Temporal alignment and $z$-score filter.}
Sessions are partitioned into $G{=}30$-second bins.
Per-bin mean confusion $\bar{c}_k$ and strategy quality label
$q_k\!\in\!\{\text{high},\text{low},\text{unknown}\}$ are computed by
majority vote over utterances overlapping bin $k$.
A bin is flagged as a \emph{friction candidate} when
\begin{equation}
  \bar{c}_k > \mu_C + z\,\sigma_C
  \quad\text{and}\quad q_k \neq \text{high},
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_C$, $\sigma_C$ are the session-level mean and standard
deviation of $\{\bar{c}_k\}$, and $z$ is a sensitivity parameter
(default $z{=}1.0$; lowered to $z{=}0.3$ for the smoke test).
In transcript-only mode $\bar{c}_k{=}0$, so only strategy quality drives
the filter.

\textbf{VLM fusion (optional).}
For each candidate, Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{qwen25vl} receives 1--4 uniformly
subsampled frames, a confusion summary, strategy label, and transcript
excerpt.  It returns structured JSON with three fields:
\texttt{friction}\,(bool), \texttt{rationale}\,(free-text explanation),
and \texttt{alternative\_strategy}\,(concrete teaching suggestion).
The full prompt is available in the public codebase.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\subsection{Qualitative Examples}

Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows three representative 30-second bins
from the AU1 lesson and the corresponding pipeline outputs.  The first
two bins are flagged as \textsc{Friction} by both annotators: the teacher
directly states a mathematical fact (\textit{``it will be 180 degrees''})
or applies correctional pressure (\textit{``just make sure they join''}),
both bypassing student reasoning.  The third bin illustrates a
\textsc{Disagreement}: the heuristic labels it \textit{unknown} (a question
is present but no low-quality keyword matches), while ELECTRA correctly
identifies it as \textit{Pressing for Reasoning}---a high-quality move.
This case demonstrates the heuristic's tendency to over-flag question-rich
discourse.

\subsection{CPU Smoke Test (AU1 Clip)}

\textbf{Setup.}
TIMSS AU1 \emph{Exterior Angles} (geometry, Australian Grade~8), 5-minute
clip.  CPU path: Python 3.9, OpenCV, DeepFace (TF~2.x), faster-Whisper
(\texttt{base}, int8).  Key hyperparameters: $r{=}2$\,Hz, $G{=}30$\,s.

\textbf{Results.}
With $z{=}1.0$, the AU1 teacher's question-based discourse
(\textit{``What did you learn?''}, \textit{``Do you agree?''}) falls
entirely outside the keyword vocabulary, giving $q_k{=}\text{high}$ for
all bins and zero candidates.  Lowering to $z{=}0.3$ with
\texttt{flag\_unknown\_strategy=True} produces \textbf{3 candidates}
at 150--240\,s where per-bin confusion peaks at 0.55--0.70
(Table~\ref{table:results}).  A second TIMSS clip (Math RUB) yielded
zero candidates due to Whisper \texttt{base} hallucinating a repeated
phrase under low audio SNR; mitigation via a larger Whisper model is
planned.

\subsection{Scale-Up: 53 TIMSS Lessons (Transcript-Only)}

\textbf{Setup.}
We processed all available TIMSS transcript \texttt{.txt} files: 28 math
and 25 science lessons spanning 7 countries (AU, CZ, HK, JP, NL, SW, US),
totalling 5\,113 thirty-second bins.  Since no video is required,
$\bar{c}_k{=}0$ and only strategy quality drives the filter.  We ran both
the heuristic annotator (\texttt{flag\_unknown=True}) and the ELECTRA
annotator (\texttt{flag\_unknown=False}) on the same corpus.

\textbf{Quantitative results.}
Table~\ref{table:results} summarises all conditions.
The heuristic flags \textbf{1\,611/5\,113 bins} (31.5\%) as risky;
ELECTRA flags \textbf{988/5\,113} (18.9\%).  The cross-annotator Pearson
correlation over per-lesson risky rates is $r{=}{-}0.35$, indicating
substantial disagreement.

Figure~\ref{fig:risky_country} shows per-country risky-bin rates.
US math lessons show the highest heuristic rate (42.9\%) yet among the
lowest ELECTRA rates (14.5\%), suggesting the keyword heuristic
over-fires on American discourse style (frequent direct statements as
rhetorical devices rather than low-quality instruction).
Figure~\ref{fig:electra_heuristic} visualises this divergence directly.

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the per-bin strategy quality timeline for
the full AU1 lesson (87 bins).  Heuristic-risky bins cluster in the
middle of the lesson where the teacher introduces definitions, consistent
with the smoke-test candidates.

Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} provides a lesson-level overview across the full
corpus.  Science lessons show lower average heuristic friction rates (28.3\%)
than math (34.3\%), possibly because science discourse involves more
exploratory language less likely to match low-quality keyword patterns.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Annotator disagreement.}
The $r{=}{-}0.35$ correlation between heuristic and ELECTRA risky rates
is the most striking finding of this study.  Two explanations are likely:
(1)~the heuristic fires on surface-level keywords (\textit{``the formula
is''}, \textit{``just copy''}) that may appear in otherwise well-structured
discourse, inflating its rate; and (2)~ELECTRA's \textit{No Move} label
does not distinguish between a teacher giving a direct explanation and one
who is simply silent---both are treated as low-quality by the heuristic
but as \textit{No Move} (not inherently risky) by ELECTRA.  Human
annotations are essential to adjudicate.

\textbf{US anomaly.}
US math lessons show the sharpest divergence: heuristic 42.9\%,
ELECTRA 14.5\%.  American math classrooms in the TIMSS corpus are
characterised by frequent declarative statements used as scaffolding
rather than instruction, a style that triggers keyword patterns without
representing low-quality teaching.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Automated facial affect inference may misread culture- or disability-related
cues; all outputs are advisory and require human review.  The confusion
proxy (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf}) uses fixed weights not validated against
ground-truth affect labels.  VLM fusion awaits GPU evaluation.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented an end-to-end multimodal pedagogical friction detector and
scaled the transcript-only pipeline to 53 TIMSS lessons across 7 countries.
The heuristic annotator flags 31.5\% of 30-second bins as risky; the ELECTRA
Talk Moves classifier flags only 18.9\%, with a cross-annotator Pearson
$r{=}{-}0.35$.  Qualitative inspection reveals cases of genuine agreement
and instructive disagreement, motivating the next milestone: human
annotations for Precision/Recall/F1 on the full AU1 lesson.
Longer term, running Qwen2.5-VL on the flagged candidates will close the
loop from detection to actionable teaching suggestions.
