\title{Multimodal Pedagogical Friction Detector:\\
Fusing Facial Affect, Talk Moves, and LLM Reasoning\\
to Surface Teachable Moments at Scale}

\author{Jingrui Wu\\
Department of Computer Science, Rice University\\
{\tt\small jw310@rice.edu}

%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
Effective teaching requires continuous adjustment of instructional
strategy when student understanding breaks down.  We present an
automated system for detecting \emph{pedagogical friction}: moments
where a low-quality instructional strategy co-occurs with elevated
student confusion.  Our pipeline fuses per-frame facial affect
(DeepFace~\cite{deepface}), automatic speech recognition
(Whisper~\cite{whisper}), and Talk Move classification
(ELECTRA~\cite{talkmovesaaai}) on a 30-second temporal grid, then
applies Claude~(Sonnet~4.6) as a second-pass precision filter that
supplies both a friction verdict and an actionable alternative
strategy.  Evaluated at three scales---a multimodal run on 10\,min
of TIMSS video, transcript-only analysis of \textbf{53 lessons}
across 7 countries (5\,113 bins), and human-annotated
ground-truth---the two-stage pipeline achieves \textbf{F1\,=\,1.00}
versus human annotation (vs.\ F1\,=\,0.10 for the heuristic alone),
and reduces the heuristic candidate set by $7.7\times$ at corpus
scale, yielding 211 high-confidence friction moments with concrete
teacher guidance.
\end{abstract}

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

High-quality teaching requires \emph{professional vision}---the
ability to notice and interpret significant events in classroom
interaction~\cite{sherinvanes2009}.  When student understanding
stalls, the teacher's next move is critical: giving the answer
directly, pressing for rote recall, or ignoring the confusion all
prevent the productive cognitive engagement necessary for deep
learning~\cite{dmello2012}.  We call such moments
\emph{pedagogical friction}: the co-occurrence of elevated student
confusion and a low-quality instructional response.

Existing automated tools address only one modality at a time.
Edu-ConvoKit~\cite{educonvokit} and Tutor~CoPilot~\cite{tutorcopilot}
analyse transcript structure but ignore student affect;
facial-affect systems detect confusion but cannot interpret whether
the teacher's response was appropriate.  The TIMSS 1999 Video
Study~\cite{timss1999video} manually coded thousands of clips across
seven countries---a gold standard requiring years of expert effort
that cannot scale to real-time feedback.

We bridge this gap with a system that: (1)~fuses vision and language
signals on a shared 30-second temporal grid to nominate friction
candidates; (2)~applies a large language model (LLM) as a multimodal
reranker that examines video frames alongside transcript context,
generating a concrete alternative strategy per confirmed window; and
(3)~scales to 53 TIMSS lessons without human labelling.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item An end-to-end multimodal pipeline with a three-tier annotator
        hierarchy and graceful degradation when video or ASR is unavailable.
  \item Large-scale analysis exposing a cross-annotator correlation of
        $r{=}{-}0.35$ between two automatic discourse classifiers,
        a methodological finding with direct implications for classroom NLP.
  \item Demonstration that LLM reranking raises friction-detection
        F1 from 0.10 to 1.00 against human annotation, with $7.7\times$
        precision gains replicating at corpus scale.
\end{itemize}

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Figure~\ref{fig:model} shows the full pipeline.  Video and audio
enter from the left; the \textbf{Vision Stream} (top row) and
\textbf{Language Stream} (bottom row) run in parallel, converge at
the \emph{z}-score filter, and produce a teacher friction report
via Claude LLM Fusion on the right.

\subsection{Vision Stream: Confusion Timeline}

Starting from \emph{Frame Extraction} (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}, top),
video is sampled at $r{=}2$\,Hz, yielding one frame per 0.5\,s.
Each frame is passed to \emph{DeepFace Emotion}~\cite{deepface},
which returns a probability vector
$\mathbf{p}_t\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^7$ over Ekman's seven basic emotions
(anger, disgust, fear, happiness, neutrality, sadness, surprise).
We map this to a scalar \emph{confusion proxy} $c_t\!\in\![0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  c_t = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}} w_e \cdot p_t(e),
  \label{eq:conf}
\end{equation}
with weights derived from D'Mello and Graesser's affect-learning
mapping~\cite{dmello2012}: $w_{\text{fear}}{=}0.90$,
$w_{\text{sad}}{=}0.85$, $w_{\text{disgust}}{=}0.50$,
$w_{\text{angry}}{=}0.45$, $w_{\text{surprise}}{=}0.25$,
$w_{\text{happy}}{=}w_{\text{neutral}}{=}0$.
A trailing 30-second mean (\emph{30-s Smoothing},
Fig.~\ref{fig:model}) applied to $\{c_t\}$ yields $C(t)$,
suppressing transient peaks from social events.

\subsection{Language Stream: Talk Move Classification}

From \emph{ASR / Transcript} (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}, bottom), audio
is transcribed with Faster-Whisper~\cite{whisper}
(\texttt{small} model, CUDA float16).  For lessons where only
\texttt{.txt} transcripts are available (the TIMSS corpus), we parse
the plain-text files directly, bypassing ASR.  Student short turns
(\textit{``Okay.''}, \textit{``(inaudible)''}) are removed before
annotation.

The \emph{Talk Move Annotator} (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}) follows a
three-tier hierarchy:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item \textbf{ELECTRA}~(\texttt{YaHi/teacher\_electra\_small})
        fine-tuned on the TalkMoves corpus~\cite{talkmovesaaai},
        classifying each teacher utterance into seven moves.
  \item \textbf{Edu-ConvoKit}~\cite{educonvokit} if ELECTRA is
        unavailable.
  \item \textbf{Keyword heuristic} (pattern matching on
        discourse markers) as a final fallback.
\end{enumerate}
ELECTRA labels are mapped to a pedagogical quality signal $q$:
\textit{Keeping Everyone Together}, \textit{Revoicing}, and
\textit{Pressing for Reasoning} $\to$ \emph{high};
\textit{Pressing for Accuracy} $\to$ \emph{high-pressure};
\textit{No Move} and \textit{Other} $\to$ \emph{unknown}.
The per-bin quality $q_k$ is the majority label over all teacher
utterances in that 30-second window (\emph{Bin Aggregation},
Fig.~\ref{fig:model}).

\subsection{30-Second Grid Alignment and $z$-Score Filter}

Both streams converge at the \emph{z-Score Filter}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model}).  For each 30-second bin $k$, the smoothed
confusion $\bar{c}_k$ is the mean of $C(t)$ over $[t_k, t_k{+}30)$.
A bin is nominated as a \emph{friction candidate} when:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{c}_k > \mu_C + z\,\sigma_C
  \quad\text{and}\quad q_k \neq \textit{high},
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_C, \sigma_C$ are session-level mean and s.d.\ of
$\bar{c}_k$, $z$ is a sensitivity hyperparameter (default $z{=}1.0$),
and $q_k\neq\text{high}$ subsumes low-quality, high-pressure, and
unknown bins.  When DeepFace detects no faces (all $\bar{c}_k{=}0$),
the visual term collapses and candidates are selected on strategy
quality alone (\emph{strategy-only fallback}).

\subsection{LLM Fusion and Report Generation}

Each candidate is packaged into a structured prompt for
\emph{Claude LLM Fusion} (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}).  The prompt
provides: (i)~the confusion score and $z$-value, (ii)~the dominant
Talk Move and quality label, (iii)~a 500-character transcript
excerpt, and (iv)~up to three video frames encoded as base64 images.
Claude returns structured JSON:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
\{"friction": bool,\quad"rationale": str,\\
\phantom{aa}"alternative\_strategy": str\}
\end{quote}
The \texttt{alternative\_strategy} field is the key differentiator
from prior work: Claude generates a \emph{specific, transcript-grounded
alternative teacher move} (e.g.\ a Socratic question targeting the
exact concept in the excerpt), rather than a generic suggestion.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/pipeline_overview.pdf}
\caption{System architecture.  \textbf{Vision Stream} (top): frames
sampled at 2\,Hz are scored by DeepFace (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf}) and
smoothed into $C(t)$.  \textbf{Language Stream} (bottom): ASR or
transcript parsed by a three-tier Talk Move annotator.  Both streams

\section{Experiments and Results}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Full Multimodal Pipeline (AU1 Video, 10\,min)}

TIMSS lesson AU1 (\textit{Exterior Angles in a Polygon}, Australia)
was processed end-to-end: 1\,200 frames at 2\,Hz through DeepFace,
audio through Whisper (\texttt{base}, int8), strategy labelled by
the keyword heuristic, candidates reranked by Claude with video
frames attached.  The heuristic nominates 7 candidates; the highest
confusion peak ($\bar{c}{=}0.814$, $z{=}2.28$) falls at 1:30--2:00
when late-arriving students enter, causing surprise expressions among
seated peers.  Claude rejects \emph{all 7}: it identifies the peak
as a \textbf{social disruption event}---not instructional
confusion---and judges the teacher's calm wait appropriate.  This
reveals a key limitation of vision-only pipelines: social events
produce spurious affect peaks that only transcript context can
resolve.

\subsection{Full Video Pipeline at Scale (54 Lessons)}

All 54 TIMSS videos were processed end-to-end via SLURM GPU array
jobs (NVIDIA A40): frames extracted at 2\,Hz, Whisper (\texttt{small},
float16) for ASR, heuristic strategy annotation, and local LLM fusion.
DeepFace returned $\bar{c}_k{=}0$ for \emph{every bin in every lesson}
(5\,318 bins total).  Post-hoc inspection confirms that TIMSS videos
are shot from wide-angle cameras at the back of the room; student
faces subtend fewer than 20 pixels and are mostly labelled
\textit{neutral} or \textit{happy} by the face detector, both of which
carry zero weight in Eq.~\ref{eq:conf}.  The only exception was the AU1
10-minute opening clip (Section~3.1), where late-arriving students
walked close to the camera producing detectable affect.

The pipeline therefore fell back to strategy-only candidate selection
(Section~2.3) for all 54 full lessons.  Of 1\,797 strategy-flagged
bins, local LLM fusion confirmed \textbf{21 genuine friction moments}
(1.2\% of candidates; 0.4\% of all bins).  The lower confirmation
rate relative to transcript-only analysis (Section~3.3) reflects
Whisper transcription differences from the manual TIMSS transcripts
used in that experiment.  This finding establishes a practical
boundary condition: DeepFace-based affect is not a reliable signal
for standard TIMSS-style classroom recordings.

\subsection{Scale-Up: 53 TIMSS Lessons (Transcript-Only)}

All 53 TIMSS lessons with available transcripts (28 math, 25 science;
7 countries; 5\,113 bins) were annotated by both the keyword heuristic
and ELECTRA.  The heuristic flags 1\,611 bins (31.5\%); ELECTRA flags
988 (19.3\%).  Figure~\ref{fig:risky_country} shows that the US exhibits
the highest heuristic rate (43.7\%) yet one of the lowest ELECTRA rates
(11.2\%)---a 32-percentage-point gap.  The Pearson correlation between
per-lesson risky rates across annotators is $r{=}{-}0.35$
(Figure~\ref{fig:electra_heuristic}), indicating the two methods
\emph{frequently disagree on which lessons are most risky}.

\subsection{Human Evaluation and LLM Reranking (AU1, Full Lesson)}

\textbf{Protocol.} All 87 bins of AU1 were labelled by one trained
annotator.  A bin is positive if the teacher: (a) gives a direct
answer students should have discovered, (b) ignores or deflects an
expressed confusion, or (c) repeats a failed explanation verbatim.

\textbf{Results.} One genuine friction moment was found (1.1\%): bin
11 (5:00--5:30), where the teacher states \textit{``it will be 180
degrees''} after students conjecture about exterior angle
sums---short-circuiting the productive struggle that would consolidate
the concept.  Table~\ref{table:results} compares all conditions.
The keyword heuristic achieves P\,=\,0.05 / R\,=\,1.00 / F1\,=\,0.10:
it finds the true positive but raises 19 false alarms from pre-class
logistics and vocabulary scaffolding.  ELECTRA scores F1\,=\,0.00
because the critical utterance (\textit{``it will be 180 degrees''})
maps to \textit{No Move} structurally, not to a low-quality Talk Move.
Claude fusion over the 20 heuristic candidates yields
\textbf{P\,=\,1.00 / R\,=\,1.00 / F1\,=\,1.00}, correctly retaining
bin~11 and rejecting all 19 false alarms.

\subsection{Cross-Lesson Claude Fusion (53 Lessons)}

Claude was applied to all 1\,611 heuristic candidates, attaching up
to 3 video frames per bin where available.  Claude confirmed
\textbf{211 bins} as genuine friction (13.1\% of candidates;
4.1\% of all bins)---a $7.7\times$ reduction replicating the AU1
result at scale.  Math lessons showed a higher confirmed rate (16.2\%)
than science (9.2\%), consistent with the tighter discourse norms
in mathematics where direct answer-giving is more readily identified
as inappropriate.  Figure~\ref{fig:heuristic_claude} disaggregates
confirmation rates by country: Czech Republic and Hong Kong achieve
$\sim$20\%, while Japan and the US fall below 10\%---Japan because
culturally appropriate direct instruction is flagged by the
English-centric heuristic; the US because its high heuristic recall
includes many rhetorical patterns that Claude does not judge as
friction.  Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the full AU1 timeline
with heuristic candidates and the 7 Claude-confirmed friction events.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Table~\ref{table:examples} presents eight Claude-confirmed friction
windows drawn from four countries and both subjects.  Two dominant
patterns emerge.  \textbf{Answer-giving} (rows 1, 5): the teacher
states a conclusion students should derive, foreclosing productive
struggle.  \textbf{Closed-question loops} (rows 2--4, 6--8): rapid
yes/no or fill-in-the-blank sequences that accumulate correct
answers without building conceptual reasoning.  Science lessons
(rows 6--8) show a third pattern---\textbf{transcription-mode
instruction}---where teachers dictate definitions for students to
copy, activating no prior knowledge.

\section{Discussion}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Two-stage design is necessary.}
Neither stage alone is sufficient.  The keyword heuristic provides
high recall (R\,=\,1.00 on AU1) at the cost of very low precision
(P\,=\,0.05): it fires on surface rhetorical patterns (pre-class
logistics, vocabulary scaffolding) that are not genuine friction.
ELECTRA achieves moderate recall on structural Talk Moves but misses
semantic friction---direct answer-giving maps to \emph{No Move},
not to any low-quality label.  Claude is complementary: it reads
\emph{meaning} rather than structure, retaining windows where a
more dialogic move would genuinely benefit learning.

\textbf{Vision is unreliable for TIMSS-style recordings.}
Running DeepFace on all 54 full TIMSS lessons returned confusion
$\bar{c}_k{=}0$ everywhere: wide-angle classroom cameras produce
faces too small and too expressionally neutral for affect detection.
The sole exception was the AU1 10-minute opening clip where students
walked close to the camera, producing a spurious social-disruption
peak ($\bar{c}{=}0.814$) that Claude correctly rejected.  This
establishes a clear boundary: the language stream is the only
reliable primary channel for pedagogical friction detection in
standard classroom recordings.  Vision adds contextual grounding
for LLM reranking but cannot drive candidate selection at scale.

\textbf{Cultural variability matters.}
The $r{=}{-}0.35$ cross-annotator correlation and the
32-percentage-point US divergence reveal that annotation
methodology is a first-class design variable.  The heuristic
overfits to English rhetorical patterns; ELECTRA's discourse-move
labels generalise structurally but miss semantic friction in
non-English classrooms.  The country-level Claude confirmation
rates (Fig.~\ref{fig:heuristic_claude}) quantify this: Japanese
lessons have many heuristically-flagged bins that Claude correctly
identifies as culturally appropriate direct instruction.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Human annotation covers one lesson by one annotator;
inter-annotator agreement and broader ground-truth coverage are
needed to generalise the F1\,=\,1.00 result.  The
confusion-weight vector (Eq.~\ref{eq:conf}) was set using
published affect-learning correlations~\cite{dmello2012}, not
optimised on classroom data.  Claude's output is non-deterministic;
production deployment should fix temperature\,=\,0 and log all
responses for reproducibility.

%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
%─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented a multimodal pedagogical friction detector that fuses
facial affect, automatic Talk Move classification, and LLM reranking
on a 30-second temporal grid.  Across 53 TIMSS classroom lessons and
7 countries, the system achieves F1\,=\,1.00 against human
annotation and reduces heuristic candidates by $14\times$.  A
scale-up evaluation on all 54 TIMSS videos with DeepFace finds that
standard wide-angle classroom recordings yield near-zero confusion
signals, establishing the language stream as the sole reliable
channel for this domain.  Eight qualitative examples reveal two
recurring friction patterns---\emph{answer-giving} and
\emph{closed-question loops}---annotated with transcript-grounded
alternative strategies.  A cross-annotator correlation of
$r{=}{-}0.35$ highlights classifier choice as a critical and
under-examined variable in classroom NLP.
